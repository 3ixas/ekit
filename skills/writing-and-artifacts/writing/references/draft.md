# Draft

Draft from an agreed brief or infer a compact brief from the request and evidence.

## Compose

- Lead with the fact, request, scene, or tension that gives the reader a reason to continue.
- Give each paragraph one main movement and make its first sentence useful.
- Use concrete nouns and active verbs. Prefer a specific example over an abstract intensifier.
- When the writer has supplied a personal journey, motivation, or point of view, make its turning point or tension the spine of the piece. Show the resulting choices and actions instead of replacing them with a list of strengths.
- When the draft must explain fit, connect the writer's evidence to this particular reader, context, or opportunity. Do not restate the brief as generic praise.
- Choose the few details that make the writer or subject memorable. A shorter, specific account is stronger than a complete catalogue.
- Vary sentence length because the thought demands it, not to manufacture style.
- Connect claims only as strongly as the evidence permits.
- Match the form: an email should make the request and next step easy to find; an essay can earn a slower development; a post needs a sharp promise without distorting the content.
- End at the point of resolution, decision, invitation, or consequence.

## Preserve authorship

Use the author’s supplied wording, experiences, opinions, and degree of certainty as primary evidence. Apply `VOICE.md` or `STYLE.md` when present, but treat the current request as the latest authority. Do not add fake vulnerability, personal anecdotes, rhetorical questions, or provocative certainty to make the prose appear human.

## Deliver

Return the draft first. Add brief notes only for unresolved factual gaps, consequential alternatives, or assumptions the user needs to assess. Drafting does not authorize sending or publishing.
